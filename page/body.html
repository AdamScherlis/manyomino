One polyomino out of 10{{EXP}}

Join {{NHERO}} unit squares edge to edge. There are about 10{{EXP}} ways to do it. The shape below is one of them, drawn so that every one of those ways was equally likely. It comes from a random walk on shapes, and most of this page is about making that walk fast enough to believe.

The typical polyomino is a twig

What “uniformly at random” picks, and why it is never a blob.

A polyomino is a set of squares on the grid that hang together along shared edges. Count two as different if they differ by a rotation or reflection (these are fixed polyominoes) and there are 19 with four cells, 63 with five, 2,725 with eight. The count keeps multiplying by about four per added cell: it grows like 0.317 × λn / n with λ ≈ 4.0626, a number known as Klarner’s constant that nobody has a formula for.

The polyominoes people draw are solid: rectangles, chunky tetris pieces. Those are a vanishing minority. A solid shape has few ways to rearrange itself, while a thin branching one has enormously many, so when every shape gets equal weight the thin branching ones win almost every draw.

A random walk on shapes

How to sample from a set far too large to list.

You cannot enumerate 10{{EXP}} shapes and pick one. You also cannot simply grow a shape cell by cell: every natural growth rule favours some shapes over others, and the favourites are blobs. The standard answer is to start from any polyomino and change it a little at random, over and over, using a rule whose long-run behaviour is known to be uniform.

The simplest such rule moves one cell at a time. Pick a cell and a place on the boundary of the shape. If taking the cell away leaves the rest in one piece, move it there. Otherwise do nothing.

If every move is exactly as likely as its reverse, the walk spends equal time on every shape.

Moving a cell from c to s and moving it back from s to c both start from the same leftover shape, so they are offered with the same probability. The walk can also reach every polyomino from every other. Together those two facts make the uniform distribution its only resting state.

This is the chain behind the gallery this project started with. It is correct. The question is how long it takes.

Relocate. One cell (dashed) moves to an empty square beside the shape. The dark cell could not be chosen: it is load-bearing.

Transplant. A whole branch is cut at a load-bearing cell c, turned over, and re-attached at another cell c′, provided it fits there touching nothing else.

The one-cell walk has a long memory

Measuring how slowly the one-cell walk forgets.

A sample is only as good as the walk’s memory is short. The usual yardstick is the autocorrelation time τ: the number of moves after which some property of the shape, here its squared radius of gyration, has stopped resembling its earlier self. A run of length T is worth about T / 2τ independent samples and no more.

I measured τ for the one-cell walk on runs hundreds to thousands of τ long, at sizes from {{OLD_NMIN}} to {{OLD_NMAX}} cells. It grows like n{{Z_OLD}}. There is a simple reason it must be at least quadratic. Each move carries one cell from a random place to a random place, so the mass of any large limb does an unbiased random walk, one cell at a time. Shifting a limb’s mass by its own size m takes about m² of its own steps, and it only gets a share m/n of the moves: m n moves in all, which is n² for the limbs that set the overall shape. The measured exponent is higher still.

Extended to {{NHERO}} cells that law gives τ ≈ {{TAU_OLD_HERO}} moves. Even at a million moves a second, which is generous at that size, that is {{OLD_YEARS}} of computing for each independent sample.

Move a branch, not a cell

A second kind of move that changes the overall shape in one step.

Seven cells in ten are load-bearing, which is what makes the one-cell walk slow: most cells cannot move. It is also an opportunity. Remove a load-bearing cell and a whole branch comes loose. So pick the branch up, rotate or reflect it at random, and try to plug it in at a randomly chosen cell somewhere else. If it lands on empty ground and touches the rest of the shape only at its new anchor, keep it. Otherwise put it back.

The “touches only at its anchor” condition is what keeps the move honest. It guarantees that in the new shape the branch is again exactly what comes loose when its anchor is removed, so the reverse transplant exists and is proposed with the same probability. Equal forward and backward odds, uniform resting state, as before. Mixing the two kinds of move keeps both properties, and the one-cell moves still do the work transplants cannot, such as opening and closing loops.

Cut-and-paste moves of this kind are standard for lattice trees, where Janse van Rensburg and Madras introduced them in 1992. Here the shape may contain loops and the cells have no explicit bonds, which is why the contact condition is needed.

With transplants in the mix the autocorrelation time drops to about {{TAU_NEW_COEF}} moves per cell, growing like n{{Z_NEW}} over almost four decades of size. The perimeter, the number of holes and the elongation of the shape all relax at the same rate or faster. (One property turns out to be about {{CV_SLOW_RATIO}} times slower; it took a deliberate search to find, below.) A walk cannot do much better than this: it has to touch every cell at least once.

Making a transplant cheap

A move that picks up half the shape sounds expensive, and done naively it is: finding the branch means flood-filling it. But most proposed transplants fail, and usually fail within a few cells of the anchor, because the branch’s root lands on something. So the sampler floods the branch and the neighbourhood of the new anchor in lockstep, places each branch cell as it is discovered, and stops the moment a collision with something proven to be outside the branch appears. The decision is the same one the naive version would reach; the two implementations produce bit-identical runs from the same seed. With that, the cost of a move grows only like n{{COST_EXP}}, nearly all of it spent on the large branches that do get moved.

Put together, one independent sample costs about n{{CPU_EXP}} of computing: {{CPU_HERO}} on one core at {{NHERO}} cells.

What the samples say

Two measurements with known answers, and a comparison.

A sampler like this earns trust by reproducing numbers obtained in entirely different ways. Here are two that are known to high precision, and one comparison with this project’s earlier runs.

The size exponent

The radius of gyration should grow as nν. The best published estimate, from a different algorithm on shapes up to a few thousand cells, is ν = 0.6412 ± 0.0005. A straight-line fit to the sizes from {{NU_NMIN}} cells up gives ν = {{NU}} ± {{NU_ERR}}.

Klarner’s constant, by counting removable cells

Take any polyomino with n cells and add a cell on its boundary: you get one with n+1. Take one with n+1 and remove a cell that is not load-bearing: you get one with n. Those are the same set of pairs, counted from either end, so

A(n+1)A(n) = average number of empty squares bordering an n-cell shapeaverage number of removable cells in an (n+1)-cell shape

where A(n) is the number of polyominoes with n cells and both averages are over uniform samples.

The left side tends to λ. So the growth rate of a count that is known exactly only for a few dozen sizes can be read off two local statistics of a big sample. It is also a severe test: a sampler with any bias towards thin or fat shapes gets it wrong. Series analysis of exact counts gives λ = 4.0625696. Pooling the sizes from {{LAM_NMIN}} cells up, with one extra-long run at 2,000 cells, the samples give λ = {{LAM}} ± {{LAM_ERR}}.

Could the samples still be subtly wrong?

Everything above assumes that by the time a shape is recorded, the walk has forgotten where it started. The evidence so far is the memory of a single number, the radius. A walk can pass that test and still fail: if some other property of the shape relaxed far more slowly, every sample would be slightly atypical in a way the radius never shows. No theorem rules this out for this walk, so the claim has to be attacked from outside. Three attacks follow.

References that have nothing to forget

First, a way to look at a shape from many sides: {{CV_NOBS}} measurements covering its overall geometry (every moment of its mass up to sixth order, how the mass is spread radially), its fine grain (each local pattern, holes, loops) and its skeleton (which cells hold it together, how it divides at its centre, the length of its trunk, distances measured along the shape). Then three sources of shapes that involve no walk at all.

  • Every shape.At 14 and 16 cells a computer can list them all, {{CV_COUNT14}} and {{CV_COUNT16}} of them, and average each measurement exactly.
  • Exact clusters.Colour each square of a grid black with probability p. The black cluster around a chosen square, on the occasions it has exactly n cells, is an exact draw from a known distribution: each shape weighted by (1 − p) to the power of its perimeter. The walk can be set to sample that distribution instead of the uniform one. Growing clusters until one stops at the right size is slow, but each is independent of the last: {{CV_EXACT60}} at 60 cells, {{CV_EXACT250}} at 250, {{CV_EXACT500}} at 500, {{CV_EXACT1000}} at 1,000.
  • Grown populations.For uniform shapes, 200,000 clusters are grown side by side, a cell at a time. Each carries a weight that corrects for how it was grown; heavy ones are cloned, light ones culled. The weights also add up to an estimate of how many polyominoes exist, which is known, so this reference checks itself.

Each dot below is one measurement in one test: the walk’s average minus the reference’s, in units of the standard error of that difference. If the walk is right, the dots in a row should scatter like a bell curve of width one.

The grown populations also show what a failing sampler looks like. Their count of polyominoes comes out within {{CV_GCOUNT250}} of the known value at 250 cells and {{CV_GCOUNT500}} at 500. At 1,000 cells it is {{CV_GCOUNT1000}}, and at 2,000, {{CV_GCOUNT2000}}: by then each population descends from only a few hundred early ancestors, and its averages drift away from the walk’s (the two starred rows). The walk has a check of the same kind, Klarner’s constant above, and passes it at every size. So the starred rows say the reference failed, not the walk; but it also means that beyond 1,000 cells there is no independent reference left.

A search for the slowest property

The second attack goes after the slow property directly. For each of the {{CV_NOBS}} measurements, at sizes up to {{CV_SLOW_NMAX}} cells, how long does the walk remember it? Most are forgotten as fast as the radius or faster. One family is not: the length of the shape’s trunk, counted as the cells whose removal would split off at least 1% of the shape. It takes about {{CV_TAU_SINGLE}} moves per cell to forget, against {{CV_TAU_RG}} for the radius.

Then a search over combinations. A standard method finds the weighted sum of all the measurements and their squares that changes most slowly along one run; its memory is then timed on a second, independent run, so that a pattern fitted to noise does not count. The slowest combinations it finds are built from the trunk again and from one other thing, the size of the largest clump of cells held together by loops. They take about {{CV_TAU_MODE}} moves per cell.

So the worry was partly right. There is a slower property, and the honest relaxation time of this walk is about {{CV_TAU_MODE}} moves per cell, not {{TAU_NEW_COEF}}. No measured average changes, because the error bars never relied on the radius’s time: they come from the scatter of blocks at least 70 moves per cell long. What changes is the accounting. An independent sample costs about {{CV_SLOW_RATIO}} times the figure quoted earlier. The {{BURN_N}} moves per cell discarded at the start of each run are about {{CV_BURN_SLOW}} relaxation times, not 20. And the {{NHERO}}-cell runs are about {{NEFF_HERO_SLOW}} relaxation times long after burn-in, not {{NEFF_HERO}}. {{CV_SLOW_TREND}}

What is and is not established

  • Exactly uniform on small shapes.For every size up to 9 cells the walk visits all of the fixed polyominoes (9,910 of them at n = 9) with frequencies that pass a chi-square test against uniform, for each kind of move and for the mixture. At 14 and 16 cells its averages of all {{CV_NOBS}} measurements match exact enumeration, each to within {{CV_ENUM_PREC}}% of that measurement’s spread.
  • Every move can be undone.A self-test replays the reverse of each accepted move and checks that it is accepted and restores the shape. Across four million relocations and half a million transplants, at sizes from 60 to 64,000 cells, it never failed.
  • The fast implementation is the slow one.The lockstep transplant and a short reference version make identical decisions: same seed, same trajectory, bit for bit, at 30, 300 and 3,000 cells.
  • Independent runs agree.{{AGREE}}
  • How the big ones start.Each size starts from a finished shape a quarter as large, scaled up 2×, and then runs on. The scaled-up shape has the right overall form but is made of 2×2 blocks; that fine structure takes about 40 moves per cell to decay, so the first {{BURN_N}} moves per cell of every run (and never less than a fifth of it) are thrown away.
  • Agrees with samplers that have no memory.Up to 500 cells for uniform shapes and 1,000 cells for percolation-weighted ones, on all {{CV_NOBS}} measurements (above).
  • Not established: a proof of fast mixing.The relaxation times are measurements. The search for slow properties covered {{CV_NOBS}} measurements and their combinations at sizes up to {{CV_SLOW_NMAX}} cells; a property outside that list could still be slower. Beyond 1,000 cells no independent sampler is available, so the larger sizes rest on the walk’s own internal checks. The largest size rests on about {{NEFF_HERO_SLOW}} relaxation times of running after burn-in, across two chains.
